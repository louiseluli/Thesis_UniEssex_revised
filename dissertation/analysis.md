> *How are videos currently categorised, and what potential biases exist in the data?*

### 1.1 Who is in the corpus?

The corpus contains **535,236 videos** collected via the Redtube API across 111 categories. Group membership is inferred from user-submitted tags and categories using a lexicon-based one-hot encoding with priority-ordered assignment (Black Women → White Women → Asian Women → Latina Women → Other).

The long-tail structure is immediate: 86.2% of the corpus carries no demographic classification. Of the 13.8% that does, White Women are twice as prevalent as any other group. This imbalance is not uniform noise — it reflects structural features of the platform economy.

## 3. Fairness Evaluation Framework — RQ3

> *How do we quantify and compare biases across systems?*

### 3.1 Metrics

Three complementary metrics, applied identically across all models:

- **EOD (Equal Opportunity Difference):** White Women TPR − Group TPR. Primary metric because the harm is specifically about recall — missed positive content means invisible creators.
- **Accuracy Disparity:** Overall accuracy gap. A softer metric; groups can achieve high accuracy while suffering large recall gaps.
- **Precision Disparity:** White Women precision − Group precision. Monitors over-triggering (false positives imposing noise on minority groups).

### 3.2 AUROC and discrimination vs calibration

## 4. Mitigation Methods — RQ4

> *What bias mitigation techniques are most effective?*

### 4.1 Pre-processing: Reweighing (Kamiran–Calders 2012)

**What it does.** Before training, each training instance is assigned a weight based on its (group, label) cell:

$$w(a, y) = \frac{P(A=a) \cdot P(Y=y)}{P(A=a, Y=y)}$$

Instances in underrepresented cells (e.g., Black Women × Amateur=1) get weight > 1; overrepresented cells get weight < 1. The RF is trained with `sample_weight=w`. The model architecture, features and hyperparameters are identical to the baseline — only the effective training distribution changes.

**Why it works.** The baseline model was undertrained on minority groups because they were underrepresented. Correcting the weight distribution improves the decision boundary for *all* groups simultaneously — White Women's recall also increases (0.758→0.930).

**Results (test set):**

| Metric | RF Baseline | Reweighing | Change |
|:-------|------------:|-----------:|-------:|
| Accuracy | 0.878 | **0.953** | +7.5pp |
| F1 | 0.758 | **0.915** | +15.7pp |
| Black Women EOD | 0.196 | **0.092** | **−53%** |
| Latina Women EOD | 0.200 | **0.077** | **−61%** |
| Asian Women EOD | 0.109 | 0.099 | −9% |

*Source: `outputs/data/10_reweigh_overall_metrics.csv`, `outputs/data/10_reweigh_disparities.csv`*

**Per-group performance:**

| Group | Recall (Baseline) | Recall (Reweighed) | EOD |
|:------|------------------:|-------------------:|----:|
| White Women | 0.758 | 0.930 | — |
| Black Women | 0.562 | **0.838** | 0.092 |
| Latina Women | 0.558 | **0.853** | 0.077 |
| Asian Women | 0.649 | **0.831** | 0.099 |

*Source: `outputs/data/10_reweigh_group_metrics.csv`*

### 4.2 In-processing: Exponentiated Gradient + Demographic Parity (EG + DP)

**What it does.** Fairlearn's `ExponentiatedGradient` wraps a `LogisticRegression` classifier and iteratively adjusts a Lagrange multiplier penalising violations of the **Demographic Parity** (DP) constraint. DP requires equal positive *prediction rates* across groups regardless of the true label:

$$P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a) \approx P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a')$$

**Why the constraint choice matters.** DP is not EOD. DP does not condition on Y=1 — it equalises prediction rates unconditionally. In a dataset where the positive class (Amateur) is semantically entangled with group membership through the vocabulary structure, forcing equal prediction rates does not produce equal true positive rates.

**Results (test set):**

| Metric | RF Baseline | EG + DP |
|:-------|------------:|--------:|
| Accuracy | 0.878 | 0.878 |
| Recall (overall) | 0.677 | **0.914** |
| Precision (overall) | 0.862 | **0.726** |
| Black Women EOD | 0.196 | 0.138 |
| **Latina Women EOD** | **0.200** | **0.297** ⚠️ |

*Source: `outputs/data/11_inproc_overall_metrics.csv`, `outputs/data/11_inproc_disparities.csv`*

<picture>
  <source media="(prefers-color-scheme: dark)" srcset="../outputs/figures/eval/11_inproc_margins_dark.png">
  <img alt="EG in-processing margins" src="../outputs/figures/eval/11_inproc_margins_light.png">
</picture>

**Why Latina Women's EOD worsens.** The DP constraint forces the model to predict positive at the same rate for Latina Women as for White Women. To do this, it must lower the threshold (predict positive more aggressively) for Latina content. But Latina content's vocabulary is heterogeneous — many Latina-positive predictions are false positives. The high-recall, low-precision regime the model enters achieves equal prediction rates but *worse* equal opportunity. The model has solved the stated problem (DP) and failed the unstated one (EOD).

**This is not a tuning failure — it is the wrong objective for this data.** DP is only equivalent to EOD when base rates are equal across groups, which they are not in this corpus (Latina Women have a 31% Amateur positive rate vs 28% for White Women).

---

### 4.3 Post-processing: ThresholdOptimizer (Equalized Odds)

**What it does.** Fairlearn's `ThresholdOptimizer` with **Equalized Odds** constraint. The fitted RF baseline's predicted probabilities are used unchanged — no retraining. On the validation set, a linear programme finds per-group decision thresholds that minimise EOD violation subject to accuracy constraints. Each group receives a different cutoff:

- **White Women:** threshold raised (currently over-classified relative to fairness target)
- **Black / Latina / Asian Women:** threshold lowered (currently under-classified)

**Results (test set):**

| Metric | RF Baseline | ThresholdOpt | Change |
|:-------|------------:|-------------:|-------:|
| Accuracy | 0.878 | 0.839 | −3.9pp |
| F1 | 0.758 | 0.693 | −6.5pp |
| Black Women EOD | 0.196 | **0.008** | **−96%** |
| Latina Women EOD | 0.200 | **0.025** | **−87%** |
| Asian Women EOD | 0.109 | **0.027** | **−75%** |

*Source: `outputs/data/12_postproc_overall_metrics.csv`, `outputs/data/12_postproc_disparities.csv`*

<picture>
  <source media="(prefers-color-scheme: dark)" srcset="../outputs/figures/eval/12_postproc_margins_dark.png">
  <img alt="Post-processing margins" src="../outputs/figures/eval/12_postproc_margins_light.png">
</picture>

**The accuracy cost** (−3.9pp) is the price of the parity guarantee. Latina Women's precision disparity is −0.032 (negative), meaning Latina Women achieve marginally *higher* precision than White Women — a slight over-correction that falls within measurement noise but confirms the optimizer is operating at the boundary of what the RF's probability estimates can support.

**Key insight.** ThresholdOptimizer achieves near-exact parity without retraining. Any deployment that has a fitted model with calibrated probabilities can apply post-processing immediately. The constraint (Equalized Odds) is the right one for this task because it conditions on Y=1 — it directly targets the recall gap that constitutes the economic harm.

| RF Reweighed | **0.953** | 0.901 | ✅ | Best accuracy; −53% Black EOD |
| BERT | 0.924 | 0.817 | ❌ | Dominated by Reweighing on both axes |
| RF Baseline | 0.878 | 0.800 | ❌ | Dominated |
| EG + DP | 0.878 | 0.703 | ❌ | **Worst fairness despite explicit constraint** |

*Source: `outputs/data/25_pareto_points.csv`, `outputs/data/mitigation_effectiveness.csv`*

**Two and only two strategies are non-dominated:**

1. **Reweighing** — choose this when accuracy matters and retraining is feasible. It improves accuracy for all groups simultaneously and is validated as robust to sample-size artefacts (10b).

2. **ThresholdOptimizer** — choose this under a regulatory parity mandate, when retraining is not possible, or when the policy requirement is that recall rates must fall within a defined tolerance. The 3.9pp accuracy cost buys a near-exact parity guarantee.

**EG + DP is actively harmful** — it achieves neither good accuracy nor good fairness and is dominated on both dimensions. It is the clearest demonstration that the choice of fairness constraint (DP vs EOD) is not a technical detail: it is the primary determinant of whether an intervention works.

## 7. Where the Research Goes Next

### 7.1 Multi-platform auditing at scale

This pipeline was designed to be portable. The same methodology — corpus construction, lexicon-based intersectional group inference, fairness metric suite, Pareto analysis — can be applied to any platform that exposes metadata. Immediate targets:

- **OnlyFans / Fansly**: creator-platform fee structures; demographic distribution of subscription income
- **MindGeek portfolio (PornHub, YouPorn, RedTube cross-platform)**: compare algorithmic structures across owned platforms
- **YouTube / TikTok**: the same Amateur/professional pipeline segregation exists; racial gaps in monetisation eligibility have been documented but not formally audited with this framework

The key bottleneck is data access, not methodology. The Redtube API is unusually open. For other platforms, researcher access agreements or platform cooperation would be required.

### 7.2 Longitudinal fairness — beyond a snapshot

The slope gap finding (−3.38 rating-points/year) reveals a gap the current framework cannot close: a model that is fair today will drift toward unfairness if the underlying platform dynamics continue. Future work should:

- **Define temporal fairness metrics**: fairness evaluated not at a point in time but across a model's deployment lifetime
- **Design retraining schedules** informed by the expected drift rate — the slope gap provides a principled basis for estimating how quickly a fair model becomes unfair
- **Develop early-warning monitors**: track group-level EOD monthly and trigger retraining when EOD exceeds a defined threshold (e.g., 0.15)

### 7.3 Moving beyond inferred attributes

All group labels in this corpus are inferred from user-submitted metadata, not self-reported. This creates two problems:

1. **Circularity**: the model learns racial categories from the same metadata it is supposed to classify — the inference and the target are not independent
2. **Creator-identity mismatch**: a video tagged "ebony" may or may not be produced by a Black creator; the metadata describes the platform's categorisation, not the creator's identity

Future work should explore:
- **Privacy-preserving self-identification systems**: opt-in demographic disclosure with strong anonymisation guarantees
- **Disaggregated inference error analysis**: measuring how often the lexicon-based group assignment is wrong, and what the downstream fairness implications of misclassification are
- **Third-party annotation**: structured human annotation of a gold-standard subset to validate inference quality (the `27_ground_truth.py` template is already built into this pipeline)

### 7.4 Debiasing at the metadata layer

The linguistic harm is embedded in the tag infrastructure. Model-level interventions cannot address it. Several upstream approaches merit investigation:

- **Tag vocabulary debiasing**: modify the tag recommendation system to reduce the correlation between racial-identity tags and platform engagement signals
- **Category reassignment audits**: systematically review whether racialised content is being assigned to categories that carry structural disadvantages (lower search ranking, different monetisation tiers)
- **Platform-level natural experiments**: the introduction of a new category or tagging policy creates a quasi-experimental setting for measuring causal effects of metadata changes

### 7.5 Multi-label fairness

The positive-class definition (Amateur vs not) imposes a binary frame on a multi-label corpus. A video can be Amateur AND Asian AND Blonde simultaneously. Standard fairness metrics evaluate single-label tasks. Future work should:

- **Develop multi-label EOD**: extend equal opportunity metrics to settings where the positive class is not exclusive
- **Study label co-occurrence bias**: whether protected-group videos are disproportionately assigned to lower-engagement label combinations
- **Model the label correlation structure**: the category co-occurrence network (step 20) provides the foundation for this analysis

### 7.6 Regulatory framework development

No current algorithmic accountability framework mandates intersectional fairness auditing for adult content platforms. The EU AI Act (2024) covers hiring, credit, education — not content classification. The DSA imposes transparency obligations but does not specify fairness metrics. The UK Online Safety Act addresses illegal content and child safety.

The audit methodology developed in this dissertation is a candidate for regulatory standardisation:

- **Audit components**: corpus construction protocol, group inference validation, fairness metric suite (EOD, accuracy disparity, precision disparity), Pareto evaluation
- **Reporting requirements**: group-level performance tables, temporal trend monitoring, intervention comparison
- **Trigger thresholds**: the 10b matched-N sensitivity check provides a methodology for validating that reported improvements are real

Translating this into a regulatory standard requires engagement with platform operators, creator advocacy groups, and policymakers — all of whom currently have no agreed-upon metric for what "fair" means in this context.

### 7.7 Creator-centred economic evaluation

Current metrics measure model performance, not creator income. The IPTW analysis bridges this gap partially (the Amateur premium is a measurable economic quantity), but future work should:

- **Link classification errors to income proxies**: use platform API data to estimate the monetisation impact of missed Amateur labels at the creator level
- **Study creator retention**: does systematic mislabelling cause creators to leave the platform? Are exit rates higher for racialised groups?
- **Measure the compounding effect**: the three harm types (classification, temporal, linguistic) operate simultaneously — what is the joint economic impact over a 3-year creator career?

### 7.8 Adversarial robustness of fair models

The group priority fix revealed that model behaviour is sensitive to how groups are defined. Fair models trained under one grouping scheme may behave differently under another. Future work should:

- **Stress-test the group definition**: what happens to reweighing's fairness guarantees when group inference errors are 5%, 10%, 20%?
- **Evaluate distributional shift**: the corpus is a snapshot; platform content evolves. How quickly do the calibrated thresholds (ThresholdOptimizer) become miscalibrated as the distribution shifts?
- **Study platform-adaptive gaming**: if creators learn that certain tags improve classification outcomes, does the tag distribution shift in ways that re-introduce bias?
